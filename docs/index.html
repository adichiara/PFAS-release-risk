Is there PFAS in Massachusetts drinking water?

PFAS, the "forever chemicals," have been found in drinking water across the state. Here is what public records show about the water people actually drink, and what you can do.

The short answer: for most people on public water, PFAS levels are low or not detected, and they have dropped sharply as water systems added treatment. About half of people on public water have no detectable PFAS in their tap water.

The biggest unknown is private wells. About 1.0 million people drink from private wells, which nobody tests unless the owner does.

What are PFAS?

PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are a large family of human-made chemicals used since the 1950s in nonstick cookware, stain- and water-resistant fabrics and carpets, grease-resistant food packaging, and some firefighting foams. They break down extremely slowly, so they build up in water, soil and people. Most people have some PFAS in their blood.

Long-term exposure to some PFAS has been linked to health effects, including changes in cholesterol, effects on the immune system and on infant growth, and some cancers. Drinking water is one of the main ways people are exposed where it is contaminated; showering and washing are much less of a concern. Pregnant and nursing women and infants are considered the most sensitive.

How much is too much?

PFAS are measured in nanograms per liter (ng/L), also called parts per trillion. One part per trillion is roughly one drop of water in 20 Olympic swimming pools.

20 ng/LMassachusetts standard (in effect since 2020) for the combined total of six PFAS, called PFAS6. This analysis measures water against this standard.
4 ng/L eachFederal limits for the two best-known PFAS, PFOA and PFOS, set by EPA in 2024. Water systems have until 2029 to comply (EPA has proposed allowing some until 2031), so these limits are stricter than the state standard and are not yet in force.

Private wells: an estimate, checked against real tests

No public data shows what is in each private well. To estimate the risk, we started from public wells, which were all tested, and found that PFAS in groundwater tends to cluster: wells near other contaminated wells are more likely to be contaminated.

We then checked that estimate against the one direct look at private wells: MassDEP's free testing of 1,649 private wells in 83 towns from 2020 to 2022, where 5% of wells were at or above the state standard. The estimate picked out the right towns (towns it ranked higher did have more contaminated wells), but it overstated the level, because public wells are more contaminated than private wells. So we adjusted it to match MassDEP's results, and in the tested towns we combined it with the town's actual test results.

The result: roughly 1 in 15 private-well users (about 70,000 people) may live where groundwater is at or above the state standard. Some areas are higher: about 17,000 people live where the estimated chance is 20% or more. Even in the lowest-risk areas, some wells are contaminated, and a test is the only way to know about yours.

Who is affected

Residents of the state's Environmental Justice neighborhoods (areas with higher shares of minority, lower-income or limited-English households) are less likely than others to have PFAS in their tap water: 42% of those on public water have detectable PFAS, compared with 54% elsewhere. Many of these neighborhoods are in cities served by MWRA. A 2022 federal review by the U.S. Government Accountability Office reached the same conclusion for Massachusetts.

Check your area

  1. Find your water source. If you pay a water bill, you are on public water; your bill or your supplier's annual water quality report (the Consumer Confidence Report) names the water system. If you have no water bill, you most likely have a private well.
  2. Open the map and find your home. Public water areas are colored by the system's measured PFAS; hover or tap for its name and levels, now and at their worst. Purple areas are private-well areas, shaded by the estimated chance of high PFAS; use the panel to hide public water and see private wells only.
  3. Look up your system's details in the detailed findings, or download the list of every water system.

What you can do

If you are on public water

  • Read your water system's annual water quality report, or ask your supplier about its PFAS results and any treatment plans.
  • If you want extra protection (for example while pregnant, nursing or preparing infant formula), a home filter certified to NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 for PFAS can reduce levels. Check that the maker has independent test results.
  • Showering, bathing and laundry are not a significant PFAS concern.

If you have a private well

  • Test it. MassDEP recommends testing all private wells for PFAS, especially within 1 to 2 miles of a known source (such as an airfield, firefighting training area, certain factories or waste sites) or of other water supplies where PFAS was found.
  • Use a MassDEP-certified laboratory (EPA methods 533 or 537.1). If nothing is detected, retest about every 10 years.
  • If PFAS is found, options include bottled water for drinking and cooking, or a treatment system (activated carbon, ion exchange or reverse osmosis). See MassDEP's private well FAQ.

How we did this

  1. Public water: every community water system in Massachusetts had to test its treated water for PFAS under the 2020 state standard. We took each system's results from the state's public data, and for towns that buy their water (like the MWRA communities), used their supplier's results. EPA's own national testing (2023 to 2025) broadly agrees.
  2. Private wells: we learned how PFAS in groundwater relates to its surroundings from about 1,030 tested public wells, then applied that to areas outside public water service.
  3. People: we matched every census block (the smallest area the Census counts) to its water system or to private wells, then counted residents, including by the state's Environmental Justice designations.
What this can and can't tell you
  • Public water levels are averages for a whole system. Levels can differ between neighborhoods served by different wells, and from year to year.
  • "Now" means each system's most recent year of results. Some small systems test less often after low early results, so their latest results may be from 2021 or 2022.
  • The private-well estimate is for areas, not individual wells. It is adjusted to MassDEP's private-well testing, which mostly invited wells near suspected sources, so it may still run somewhat high.
  • This covers drinking water only, not food, consumer products or other ways people are exposed to PFAS.
  • Results are based on the state's public data as of 2026-09-21.
